TACTICAL OVERVIEW

Relay chain — Korpiharju, northwest of Ahola

This op happens well northwest of Ahola, around the forest ridge locals call Korpiharju — not on top of Op 1's ground. Adjust markers once Eden placement is locked in.

OP 2

THE QUIET LINE

Take down a Russian relay chain strung through the forest ridge around Korpiharju, linking ground patrols, forward observers, artillery requests and command across the sector. No drones in this world — every signal still runs on wire, radio, and human error.

PHASE 1 / 5

Tap the line

Start from an FDF listening post at the treeline and scout a cable hut by a lock. Tap a field telephone line for frequencies, passwords, and — if you're patient — the QRF's route.

PHASE 2 / 5

The quarry relay

Follow the cable to an isolated quarry holding a mobile repeater and its security detail. Sabotage it quiet or hit it hard — either way, the code cylinder off the technician comes with you.

PHASE 3 / 5

Hilltop node

Move up a dry streambed to the main relay post on the high ground. Take down the antenna, the generator, the encryption set — and the orders sitting on the desk inside.

PHASE 4 / 5

One false transmission

The transmitter has one call left in it before it goes dark for good. Send a single false ground report — a phantom FDF build-up, a fake evacuation route, or a false withdrawal. Choose carefully: the whole sector reacts to whichever one you pick.

PHASE 5 / 5

Exfil through the confusion

Pull back through a line of farmsteads and a level crossing while the Russians are already redeploying a checkpoint — to the wrong sector, because of you.

COMBAT CLIMAX

They haven't given up on you yet

The main post goes dark, but the QRF hasn't fully written off your likely exfil route. A moving fight across three small points — break through a manned rail crossing checkpoint, then cut off a second vehicle patrol on the forest road. No last room to clear. Just distance, and whether they catch up before you make it.

This was never a base takedown. It's misdirection with a body count — one transmission steers how an entire sector responds for the rest of the campaign.

WHY THIS ISN'T A NEW IDEA

Cutting the wire is older than radio

Line-cutting and relay sabotage go back to partisan operations against German field-telephone networks in occupied Europe — cut the cable, and a division-sized force is suddenly running on runners and guesswork. The 44th Army Corps leans on exactly this kind of wire-and-short-hop-UHF discipline because it's harder to intercept passively than satellite comms — which is precisely why it's vulnerable to someone willing to walk up and touch it. Sending one false report over a captured transmitter is the same logic Allied deception planners used before D-Day, just at platoon scale instead of army-group scale: the enemy doesn't need fake divisions, it needs one plausible lie delivered on a channel it trusts.

INTEL

Corporate & intercepted documents

ORION INTERNATIONAL — TARGETING NOTE · RE: SECTOR RELAY CHAIN

Corps comms discipline is better than expected. Everything worth intercepting runs on cable and short-hop UHF, not satellite — which is good news, because it means the 44th goes deaf sector by sector without a single jammer switched on.

It's also bad news. Cut the wrong link at the wrong moment and they know exactly where the cut happened within the hour. This is a one-shot deception, not a standing advantage — use the transmitter once, then be somewhere else.
